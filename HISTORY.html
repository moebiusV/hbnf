HyperBNF: Return of the Metacompiler

A short history of the parser generator.

A parser generator reads a grammar — a precise description of what a language may say — and writes the program that recognizes exactly that. It matters because every compiler, every config-file reader, every network protocol has to parse text, and a parser written by hand is slow, error-prone, and thrown away whenever the language grows.

Before any of this was engineering, it was linguistics. Noam Chomsky asked how a person produces and understands sentences never spoken before, and answered: with a grammar — a finite set of rules that generates the infinite set of legal sentences. Generative grammar turned language from examples into a formal object. It gave computing the Chomsky hierarchy, a ladder from regular to context-free to context-sensitive, and every parser in this history sits on one rung of that ladder, almost always the context-free one. The idea that a grammar is a machine for generating strings is Chomsky's; everything that follows is the story of people building that machine.

A grammar has two readers: the person and the machine. Every system in this history leans toward one of them — easier for a person to read, or easier for a machine to turn into code. The aim underneath never changes: get the writer's intention into the machine without losing it, and make plain to the next reader what the machine was meant to do. One is a person talking to a machine. The other is a person talking to a person.

A language used to be described the way a recipe is: prose and examples. Two implementors reading the same manual could ship two different languages. That stopped in 1959, when John Backus needed to describe ALGOL 58 well enough that implementations would not silently diverge. He took the idea of production rules from Emil Post and published the syntax as metalinguistic formulas. The notation was rough — a colon, the word "or", brackets that did not quite close — and it said nothing about meaning, only about form. It was still the break: a finite set of rewrite rules that generated exactly the legal strings.

Peter Naur made it usable. Editing the ALGOL 60 Report, he kept Backus's idea and fixed the marks: angle brackets for non-terminals, ::= for definition, | for choice. Backus had written a paper; Naur had written the reference grammar of a real language, with recursion and block structure inside the same formalism. Donald Knuth insisted it be called Backus–Naur Form and not Backus Normal Form, because it is not a normal form. The name stuck, and the notation became the default way to write a language down.

The first compiler compilers

BNF was meant to be read, and it turned out to be nearly executable. Within a year of the ALGOL 60 Report, Edgar Irons published *A Syntax-Directed Compiler for ALGOL 60* (1961): the parser was driven straight off the BNF Naur had just written, the grammar supplying the recognition tables as a program supplies its data. The notation was written for people; it happened to run.

The name "compiler compiler" came next, from R. A. Brooker and D. Morris at Manchester (1963). Their Compiler Compiler read a phrase-structure description of a language — BNF with the semantics hung off the rules — and generated a machine-code compiler for the Atlas. It was a working tool, not a toy; it built compilers for Algol and Atlas Autocode.

A program describing itself came earlier still. John McCarthy's Lisp paper (1960) defined the language with a metacircular evaluator — eval written in Lisp, a self-interpreter — four years before META II. McCarthy meant it "for reading, not for computing"; Steve Russell had to run it for it to become a language.

Running it is where the half-page stops being a definition. Kragen Sitaker, who went on to write StoneKnifeForth, translated the Lisp 1.5 metacircular interpreter into a low-level language in 2007 and found that about half the code went to things the metacircular interpreter says nothing about: memory management, argument evaluation order, laziness versus strictness, the rest of control flow, the representation and comparison of atoms, the representation of pairs, parsing, type checking and type testing, recursive call and return, tail calls, and lexical versus dynamic scoping. The gap is not an oversight in McCarthy's eval; it is what self-definition is. Reynolds, revisiting his own definitional interpreters in 1998, put it plainly: a metacircular interpreter "is not really a definition, since it is trivial when the defining language is understood, and otherwise it is ambiguous" — his Interpreters I and II say nothing about order of application. He quotes Jim Morris going further: "The activity of defining features in terms of themselves is highly suspect, especially when they are as subtle as functional objects. It is a fad that should be debunked." Reynolds then grants what the thing is good for: he remembers McCarthy's definition as a great help when he first learned Lisp, "but it was not the sole support of my understanding."

Sitaker's conclusion is the one that bears on a compiler compiler: a metacircular compiler forces you to confront that complexity, because it has to emit the memory management and the calling convention rather than inherit them, and it is self-sustaining in a way an interpreter is not — once it runs, features added to the language are available to the compiler itself. That is the line this story follows from here: not a description that reads well, but one that has to produce the code.

Then the metacompilers. Dewey Val Schorre's META II (1964) wrote a language as "syntax equations" in the shape of BNF and compiled each equation to the subroutine that recognized it; META II compiled itself, the first documented metacompiler.

Its notation is worth reading closely, because most of it is still in use. Schorre dropped Naur's angle brackets and ::= for ordinary algebraic punctuation — a bare = between the name and its definition, / between alternatives, each equation closed by a terminator — and added the one operator BNF lacked. A top-down parser cannot take BNF's left-recursive <list> ::= <item> | <list> <item> without running off its own stack, so META II wrote repetition out as an operator instead: $ for "zero or more", with ( ) to group what it repeated. Four built-in recognizers covered the token level — .ID, .NUMBER, .STRING, and .EMPTY for a production that matches nothing — and .OUT('…') emitted a line of target assembly from inside the equation, with * standing for whatever token had just matched. The whole of an expression translator is one line:

EXPR = TERM $( '+' TERM .OUT('ADD') / '-' TERM .OUT('SUB') );

Read that and you are reading EBNF a decade early: the =, the explicit repetition operator in place of recursion, the parenthesised group. Wirth's EBNF kept the first two and spelled the repetition { }; ABNF kept the = and the /, and wrote repetition as a prefix count. The inheritance is the decision rather than the character — that a grammar for a top-down parser says "repeat this" instead of naming itself again — and every notation since has made it, hbnf included.

What did not survive into the parser generators is the part that made META II a compiler-compiler rather than one of them: output directives living inside the syntax. Yacc put its actions at the end of a production, ANTLR and its successors built a tree and walked it afterwards, and hbnf did the same — a %action{ } block had to end its rule, so there was nowhere to put .OUT where META II puts it. That is being repaired rather than admired: the block positions are a notation decision in RFCPLAN.md step 12, and %emit{ } — the generative sibling of %scan{ } and %action{ } — is the .OUT directive sixty years on, one level up, writing a target language instead of one machine's assembly. Schorre's loop closed because the directives were in the grammar. Whether hbnf's closes depends on the same thing.

Robert McClure's TMG (1965) did the same, at Texas Instruments; McIlroy ported it to Unix, and Ken Thompson used it around 1970 to write B — the language C grew out of — in place of the FORTRAN compiler he had set out to build.

None of them became the way compilers were written. Each was a demonstration tied to one machine, with a notation of its own and no settled algorithm underneath: the grammar drove the parser, but how was still open. They proved the idea. What was missing was a reliable way to do it.

Irons, IMP and the two-level grammar

Two ideas run through the early years and they pull in opposite directions: make the grammar run, and make the grammar say more.

Make it run. Irons's 1961 compiler (E. T. Irons, "A Syntax Directed Compiler for ALGOL 60", Communications of the ACM 4(1), January 1961) drove the parse straight off Naur's BNF. ALGOL 60's syntax allowed more than one parse for some programs, and Irons's answer was to add mechanisms that reject the ones that are semantically wrong (Knuth's account of the history of attribute grammars says as much). The grammar and the rule for choosing between its parses were one tool from the start. IMP (Irons; the dates differ by source, from the late 1960s into the 1970s) took the next step: the grammar is the extension mechanism. A program adds syntax statements to the language it is being compiled in, <EXP> ::= ABS ( <A> ) ::= "A<0=>-A ELSE A", and the parser, which "allows ambiguity in the syntax", settles it by rule (the 1973 Yale IMP72 Reference Manual, section 2.3.4): a terminal beats an identifier, then the reading that uses the fewest different syntax rules, then an arbitrary choice. No reserved words, one grammar for the language and for what a program adds to it. METACOMPILERS.md has the comparison with META II and SNOBOL4.

Make it say more. BNF cannot say that a name must be declared before it is used, or that the two sides of an assignment have the same type. Adriaan van Wijngaarden's answer, for the ALGOL 68 Report, was a grammar of grammars: a first level of metarules defining sets of strings (MODE :: int ; real ; ref to MODE), and a second of hyperrules whose names contain them (MODE list : MODE, comma symbol, MODE list), so that every consistent substitution of a metanotion gives an ordinary rule and one hyperrule stands for a family, usually an infinite one. With unrestricted metanotions the formalism is Turing complete.

What stopped it. By the accounts in Lindsey's A History of ALGOL 68 and Koster's The Making of ALGOL 68, the first thing was the Report itself: from the beginning, prominent members of the working group complained that two-level grammars were too complicated to understand, and the 1968 Report was called unreadable (the 1973 Revised Report is a much more readable grammar, and admits in a famous sentence that its method may be difficult for the uninitiated reader). The second was that the formalism was too strong to be used: in its full generality it cannot be the input of a parser generator, so the groups that built compilers rewrote the grammar by hand into a context-free one and checked the rest in the compiler, and the Report stopped being the specification anyone actually ran. The third was the language: large, orthogonal, with coercions and modes that made it slow to implement while Pascal and C were simple enough to compile in one pass on a small machine. The grammar did not kill ALGOL 68 alone, and the historians say that "too big" and "unreadable" are oversimplified; but a grammar nobody could read and no tool could run is a poor basis for a language that has to spread. (This paragraph is a summary of those two histories and Wikipedia's article on van Wijngaarden grammars, not of the Report; check them before quoting it.)

What hbnf takes from the line.

  • From Irons: the grammar and the choice rule are one document. IMP72's three-step tie-break is decision 14's order (a failing test removes a reading, a terminal beats an expression, the fewest rules wins, and where IMP72 says "arbitrary" hbnf says "the first written"). And an extensible, ambiguous grammar is fine if the ambiguity is detected and reported rather than silently resolved.
  • From van Wijngaarden: the idea that one rule can stand for a family (list(X) := X *( "," X )), and that two places in a rule can be made to agree (the closing tag equals the opening tag, the here-document terminator, a raw string's delimiter).
  • From both histories, what not to copy: a notation that only its author can read, and a power nobody can bound.

How hbnf makes the two-level grammar work for real. By keeping the part that is decidable and cheap, and handing the rest to tools that already exist (METACOMPILERS.md, section on W-grammars, has the argument; RFCPLAN.md has the decisions):

  • Parameterised rules over a finite set are expanded when the parser is generated, so a hyperrule becomes the ordinary rules it stands for, and the parser is the one you would have written by hand. No runtime cost, no new parsing algorithm, no undecidability. Domains are checked when the grammar is read, so a use that can never match is an error with a message. Built (tests/params.sh): see "Why a two-level grammar can fail to end" below.
  • Consistent substitution becomes a back-reference: name as tag keeps the text a name matched and $tag is that text again, in the same sequence. It is a slice of the input (two integers in the generated function, no copy), so a backtrack restores it with the position and nesting cannot mix two of them up. Mustache's closing tag, XML's, a here-document's terminator are the same rule. Built, in C (tests/backref.sh, corpus/mustache).
  • What a finite domain cannot hold, the names declared so far, is a context (decision 13: a persistent set, ? types, %bind, %scope, %where), kept local to the rule it affects. Built, in C.
  • Ambiguity is handled by local forks (decision 14, --forks) and by a lookahead test (!x, &x). Built, in C, in their local form.
  • Types and modes are checked by a pass over the tree, not by the grammar. A parser that has to be a type checker is why the Report could not be run.

The ALGOL 68 grammar will be written in this hbnf and not in the Report's literal two levels (the user's decision, 2026-10-18). ALGOL 60 came first as the proving ground, and it has been checked against the Report's own BNF with an independent recognizer.

Why a two-level grammar can fail to end, and why it almost never does

A metanotion is a set of strings defined by a context-free grammar of its own, and a hyperrule name contains metanotions, so a hyperrule is a template for every rule you get by putting a string in each. Reading a sentence means choosing those strings. Whether a choice exists is where the trouble is, because the strings are written into rule names, not into the text being read, and nothing about the finite input bounds them.

The smallest example:

A :: x ; A x.             (A is any run of x's: an infinite set)
A word : A x word.        (a hyperrule: "A word" is "A x word")

Instantiate A with x and the rule is x word : x x word; with xx, xx word : xxx word. Every instance is a rule whose only right-hand side is another instance with a longer name, and no terminal is consumed on the way. A recogniser asked whether the input holds an x word is sent to xx word, then to xxx word, and so on, though the input is a few characters long. It is left recursion, with the difference that every step is a different rule, so there is no repeated state to catch. In general, consistent substitution lets a grammar copy a string and compare it with another, and that is enough to encode a Turing machine or Post's correspondence problem. Whether a given sentence is derivable is undecidable, so no recogniser halts on every grammar and every input.

Can the cycle be detected? Often, yes, and tracking which hyperrule is being instantiated is the way. There are three cases and they differ.

  • The same instance again. Remembering the instances already made ends it exactly. When every parameter ranges over a finite set the number of instances is finite, so every chain either repeats an instance or stops (the pigeonhole principle), and the check is complete.
  • The same hyperrule with a strictly bigger argument, as above. A static check follows the chain of instances, sees each argument larger than the last with nothing consumed between, and reports it. This is the termination analysis of term-rewriting systems and Prolog programs, and it is sound but not complete: a rule whose argument shrinks for a while and then grows, or grows only after a detour through other rules, can slip through. No fixed check catches every non-terminating grammar and accepts every terminating one; that is the halting problem.
  • The same instance at the same input position, while parsing. Remembering the pair (instance, position) detects it exactly, as it does left recursion in a packrat parser.

Does laziness help? Partly, and it is how the practical parsers cope. Instantiate a metanotion only when the parser needs it at the current position, memoise by (instance, position), and the work is bounded by the input: when the input can no longer match, nothing more is made. The infinite family ref to ref to ... int is in principle unbounded but any program uses finitely many of its members. What laziness does not do is stop the cycle above, because there the demand comes from the rule itself and no input has been matched for it to run out of. The same remedy applies as for left recursion: require progress, and refuse the rule that makes none.

How hbnf spells them. As the Report does, with the operators told apart: = defines a rule, := a rule with parameters (a hyperrule, the Report's :) and ::= a domain (a metarule, the Report's ::), so level ::= 1..17 and binary(N in level) := binary(N+1) *( op(N) binary(N+1) ) read as the Report's two levels do. A metarule is a plain context-free production, over the strings that go into names instead of the sentences of the language, and here it is a finite set of numbers or words (a domain that names itself would be the infinite family, and is refused). A file in BNF, where ::= or := defines an ordinary rule, names its notation with dialect bnf; in HyperBNF's own notation a BNF rule read as a metarule is refused with the line to add.

What hbnf does.

  • Parameters range over a finite set (a number in 1..17, one of a list of words, or a rule name), so the family is finite and is expanded into ordinary rules when the grammar is read: the parser is the one you would have written by hand, with no runtime cost and no new algorithm.
  • The domain is checked then: a use outside it (binary(18) when N is 1..17) is an error that names the chain of instances that led to it, not a parse that fails on some input.
  • A chain that re-enters a hyperrule with a bigger argument is refused at once, with the chain, instead of running to a limit (there is a limit as well, at 20,000 instances).
  • Where the family is open, as Algol 68's modes are, the plan is the lazy, input-driven instantiation above, with a no-progress check; it needs support in the parser and is the Algol 68 step's work.
  • Consistent substitution is the back-reference (as, $K), and what a finite domain cannot hold, the names declared so far, is the context of decision 13. Neither needs the infinite family.

Why real grammars rarely meet it. A grammar that is written to be read, which the Report's is and every grammar a programmer writes is, has metanotions that stand for the finite things a language has: the modes a program uses, the kinds of operand, the precedence levels. Its recursion consumes input at each step, as any grammar's must if a parser is to run. The non-terminating hyperrule is a construction made on purpose, to show that the formalism is as strong as a Turing machine; none of the working descriptions or implementations (Koster's affix grammars and the CDL compiler description language, the Prolog definite clause grammars, the attribute grammars) needed more than the bounded part. Restricting parameters to finite domains, as hbnf does, makes the question decidable by construction and costs the grammars people write nothing.

Is it a typeclass? Much of it is, and the comparison is the quickest way to explain the rules to a programmer. A parameterised rule is a function from arguments to a rule, chosen by pattern: an instance declaration in Haskell is a function from types to dictionaries, chosen by pattern, and the most specific wins (binary(17) := unary beside the general binary(N in 1..17) is overlapping instances). Expanding every use before the parser runs is monomorphisation, as C++ templates and Rust generics do, and it costs nothing at run time (Haskell's dictionary passing does). A domain is a closed set of instances. And the termination trouble is the same trouble: instance resolution in Haskell can chase its own tail (instance C [[a]] => C [a]), and GHC refuses it unless UndecidableInstances is on, because its conservative check, the Paterson conditions (the constraint must be structurally smaller than the head), is a sufficient condition for finite resolution that rejects some programs that would in fact end. hbnf's growth check is the same trade. Where it differs is that nothing here is typed: a parameter is a rule name, a word or a number, not a type, and there is no inference.

What it saves. The numbers are in Measured below: the grammar text of ALGOL 60 and JSON is about half the size of the Report's BNF and the RFC's ABNF, and the OpenBSD daemons' parse.y files lose their lexers and C actions. The ALGOL 68 saving has not been measured: it is the point of the pilot, a chapter of the Report's W-grammar counted before and after.

Parameters on rules: affix grammars, CDL and Prolog

The two-level grammar was not the only answer to "BNF cannot say it". Two others, from the same decade as the Report and the one after, gave a nonterminal parameters and made the parser run them. They are the closest relatives of HyperBNF's hyperrules, and the differences are the design.

Affix grammars and CDL. Cornelis Koster, who was one of the ALGOL 68 editors, took the Report's idea and made it implementable: a nonterminal carries affixes, and each affix has a domain declared up front, a finite set of values or a structure built from them. A rule is a predicate over its affixes. It succeeds, perhaps computing values on the way out, or it fails, and the same notation describes the syntax, the checks (a name declared before it is used, two operands of one type) and the translation. CDL, the Compiler Description Language, was the working form: Koster's group wrote compilers in it, and it went through several versions (CDL2, CDL3). The domain declaration is what made it practical. A parameter that ranges over a declared set can be checked when the grammar is read, and the machine never meets the infinite family that the Report's metanotions allowed. Watt and Meijer's extended affix grammars, and AGFL (affix grammars over a finite lattice) for natural language, kept the same shape.

Prolog and the definite clause grammar. Colmerauer's metamorphosis grammars (1975) and Pereira and Warren's definite clause grammars (1980) put the same idea inside Prolog. A nonterminal is a predicate with arguments, expr(Type, Env), and the grammar is a logic program: the arguments are terms of any shape, unification passes values in both directions, and the parser is whatever Prolog's search does with it, depth-first with backtracking. That is the most general of the three, and it needs a Prolog to run. It also inherits Prolog's costs: a left-recursive rule does not end, backtracking can be exponential, a call outside the values the author meant is a failure at run time and not an error, and a parse that fails says only that it failed.

Domains are the point. All three answers separate by one choice: what a parameter may range over. The Report allowed a metanotion to be any context-free language, so a hyperrule could be an infinite family and a parse could be undecidable. Prolog allows any term, so the program can compute anything. CDL declared finite domains, and a grammar that stays inside them is checkable and terminates. Of the three, only the last gave a compiler writer a grammar that was also a guarantee.

What HyperBNF does differently, and why.

  • Domains are finite, or a counter. A parameter is a number in a range, one of a list of words, or a rule name, as in CDL, and a use outside the domain is an error that names the chain of instances that led to it. The open domain k in 0.. is a number the parser works out as it reads, for indentation and the lengths of a counted field. It is a counter and nothing else: no structure, no unification. That is what keeps every grammar decidable, and it is enough for the languages that needed more than BNF, YAML's block structure among them.
  • Values go in, and the tree comes out. An affix in CDL can be synthesised: the grammar computes. A HyperBNF parameter only restricts what parses. What a program wants from the input is the typed tree the rule shapes describe, and the program computes from that. The grammar stays a document that an RFC reader can follow, and the same file means the same thing to every backend. Names declared so far, the one thing a finite domain cannot hold, are the named context (%bind, %scope), a table beside the grammar and not a term in it.
  • Choice is ordered, so nothing backtracks without being asked. Prolog tries every way until one works; HyperBNF takes the first alternative that matches, as PEG does, and refuses a shadowed alternative when the grammar is read. There is no shift/reduce conflict to resolve and no exponential search to meet. Left recursion is read as a loop.
  • The family is expanded before the parser exists. A small table of instances becomes ordinary rules at generation time and costs nothing at run time; a large or open one is compiled as a guard on a stored number. Either way the output is the parser one would have written by hand, in C, Rust, Zig or Ada, with no engine to link. A DCG needs Prolog, and CDL needed its compiler.
  • One notation. The parameter is written on a rule in the ABNF that RFCs already use, rule(N in 1..17), beside BNF, EBNF and yacc's dialect, not in a separate formalism that has to be learned first.

The machine: Yacc

Yacc did not invent the compiler compiler; it found the algorithm that made one stick. The decade of attempts before it had proved a grammar could drive a parser, but each was a machine-specific experiment, and none had answered how to turn an arbitrary grammar into a correct parser on its own.

Stephen Johnson answered it. At Bell Labs in the early 1970s he wanted to add an exclusive-or operator to the B compiler and found the hand-written parser impossible to change cleanly. Al Aho pointed him at Knuth's LR parsing papers, and Yacc was the result. A Yacc grammar is BNF plus C code that runs when a rule matches; the tool compiles the grammar into a shift-reduce automaton. The change was in who had to understand the parser. Before Yacc, a parser was an expert's handwritten artifact. After Yacc, a grammar was a text file, and the machine did the hard part.

Yacc chose LALR(1), the compromise between canonical LR(1) — exact but explosive — and SLR(1) — small but too weak. Canonical LR(1) splits states by lookahead and blows up on a language the size of C; SLR(1) uses a global follow set and accepts reductions that are not legal in context. LALR(1) builds the LR(1) machine and merges the states that share a core, keeping the state count of LR(0) and nearly all the power of LR(1). It fit in 1970s memory, and it handled C, B, and Pascal.

What it could not handle was ambiguity. Yacc turned ambiguity into a diagnostic — shift/reduce and reduce/reduce conflicts — and gave the grammar writer tools to resolve it: shift-on-conflict for the dangling else, %left/%right/ %nonassoc for precedence. A Yacc grammar for C needed a feedback hack from the symbol table, because a typedef name and an identifier are the same token until they are not. The hack worked. It was the cost of the machine, and it stayed hidden in the grammar file for thirty years.

The protocol metalanguage: ABNF

Meanwhile the people writing network standards needed something else. RFC 822 (1982), David Crocker's specification of ARPA Internet mail, was not written for compiler authors. It was written for people who had to say, in plain ASCII, exactly which bytes a header may contain. So it dropped the angle brackets, changed ::= to =, changed | to /, and let a literal be a quoted string or a byte value. It specified a language, not a parser. There were no actions, no conflict tables, no commitment to LL or LR.

Formal grammars reached ordinary people through this door. BNF and Yacc were tools for a priesthood of compiler writers. RFC 822 was a tool for anyone who had to implement a mail client, and in the 1980s that meant nearly everyone who wrote software. A network engineer who had never heard of Chomsky could still read a rule and know what it meant. Without the RFC series, most of those people would never have seen a formal grammar at all.

It was good enough that for fifteen years every other RFC cited "the BNF in RFC 822" instead of writing its own. That citation habit produced ABNF. RFC 2234 (1997) extracted the notation and made it a standard; RFC 5234 (2008) is the current form. ABNF kept the looseness on purpose: a rule names its alternatives with /, repetition is explicit (1*DIGIT, *WSP), and the core rules (ALPHA, DIGIT, CRLF) ship with the spec. Ambiguity is legal. There is no generated automaton. ABNF exists so two protocol authors cannot silently disagree about the bytes on the wire.

Sources: RFC 822 (Crocker, 1982) · RFC 2234 (1997) · RFC 5234 (2008).

The return to top-down: ANTLR

Yacc won because it was easier than hand-writing a parser. It lost for the same reason. The shift-reduce automaton is fast, but when it rejects a grammar it tells you there is a conflict, not why, and you cannot step through the state machine it generated.

Terence Parr went the other way. ANTLR (1988–90) generated the recursive-descent parser a human would have written, top-down, with the lexer and parser in one grammar. Where finite lookahead could not decide, it added predicates and backtracking — "try this, and if it fails try that" — instead of forcing the grammar to be LALR(1). Left recursion still had to be rewritten, and backtracking could go exponential. Those were the price of a parser a person could debug.

Sources: Parr & Quong, "ANTLR: A Predicated-LL(k) Parser Generator" (1995).

The compilers: pcc, GCC, and C++

The language those compilers compiled had the same parent. C reaches ALGOL 60 through a chain of simplifications — Christopher Strachey's CPL, Martin Richards's BCPL, Ken Thompson's B, and finally Ritchie's C — the curly-brace line that kept ALGOL's block structure and dropped everything else. What C took from ALGOL 68, Ritchie said, was the scheme of type composition and its names: int, char, long, short, union, struct and void are all ALGOL 68's, the cast is named after ALGOL 68's, and the compound-assignment operators came the same way, through Douglas McIlroy's TMG. The library borrowed too: ALGOL 68's formatted output was already printf, and C kept the name and the idea of a format string — though the % directives themselves came from BCPL's writef, which had them in 1966. So the C grammar Stallman later fed to Bison was, in its bones, an ALGOL grammar.

Johnson's own compiler carried the machine into production. pcc shipped with Seventh Edition Unix in 1979, moved to the VAX through 32V, and became the compiler that let C leave the PDP-11. For a decade nearly every serious C compiler was pcc or a descendant of it.

Then came GCC. Richard Stallman wrote the C grammar — copyright 1987, GCC 1.0 shipped 22 March 1987 — and fed it to Bison rather than Johnson's Yacc. GCC 1.0 credits Stallman as author, Leonard Tower for parts of the parser and the RTL, and Paul Rubin for most of the preprocessor. Bison itself was a half-breed: Stallman wrote its C skeleton, Robert Corbett its LALR engine from his Berkeley Yacc. Michael Tiemann wrote the C++ grammar for g++, the same kind of grammar, and that one became the standing lesson in what the machine could not do.

GCC pushed pcc out. John Gilmore did the work in 1987–88, compiling the whole BSD tree with the VAX GCC so CSRG could drop pcc; Keith Bostic and Mike Karels endorsed it, for ANSI C, better code, and a way out from under the AT&T copyright. By 1994 pcc was out of the BSD line and unmaintained.

Then GCC climbed off the machine. The C++ front end dropped its Bison grammar for hand-written recursive descent in 2004; Joseph Myers wrote the C replacement for GCC 4.1 in 2006, the way Ritchie had written the original — shift and reduce became calls and returns, and the typedef hack became a real symbol table. Clang, when it came, never used a generator at all.

The one compiler that kept Yacc was pcc. Anders Magnusson revived it in 2002 from the opened 32V sources, half the front end and most of the back end rewritten. It still lives today, still driven by a Yacc grammar, and it crawls while GCC and LLVM, which do not use Yacc or Bison, are the compilers the world develops. The machine was right for 1975. It is why the work is hard now.

Sources: Ritchie, "The Development of the C Language" (1993); Johnson, pcc (1979); Stallman, GCC 1.0 (1987); Corbett, Berkeley Yacc (1985); Tiemann, g++ (1987).

Ordered recognition: PEG

Bryan Ford asked a different question. Context-free grammars were built to model natural language, where ambiguity is a feature. For a programming language it is a nuisance, and the entire apparatus — LR conflicts, scanner hacks, the lexer/parser split — exists to beat it back down.

A PEG (Ford, 2002–04) answers by changing what alternation means. In a CFG, A | B says both are possible and a conflict is an error. In a PEG, / is ordered choice: try A, and if it matches, B is never considered. Ambiguity becomes impossible because the order of the text picks the winner. The grammar is scannerless — tokens and structure live in one set of rules — and memoizing every (rule, position) pair gives linear time. Ford was explicit that he was rehabilitating a 1970 recognition scheme, not inventing one. The differences that bite are left recursion and the greed of ordered choice.

Sources: Ford, "Parsing Expression Grammars: A Recognition-Based Syntactic Foundation" (2004).

The incremental tree: Tree-sitter

The last turn was not about compilers at all. Max Brunsfeld built Tree-sitter (2014–18) for the editor: a syntax tree that updates from an edit instead of re-parsing the whole file, and that still returns something when the buffer is briefly illegal. It uses generalized LR, keeping a stack of stacks so local ambiguity can be represented, and writes ERROR and MISSING nodes into the tree rather than failing. The grammar is a JavaScript DSL that compiles to C. Atom is gone; Tree-sitter is now the usual answer whenever a tool needs a real syntax tree for a language it does not itself compile.

Sources: Brunsfeld, "Tree-sitter: A New Parsing System for Programming Tools" (2018).

What Tree-sitter does about context, and what that costs. A parser generator has three places to put context-sensitivity, and Tree-sitter uses all of them in its own way. (1) *The lexer is told what the parser expects.* It lexes by parse state: only the tokens that are valid in the current LR state can match, so < can be an operator in one state and a bracket in another, and a keyword is a keyword only where one can occur. (2) The parse forks. Where the LR table has a conflict (a * b; as a declaration or as a multiplication, T(x), a < b > c) it splits the stack and runs the readings in parallel on the same input; the ones that die are dropped, and of those that survive the one with the higher prec.dynamic and the lower error cost wins. The author names the conflicts (conflicts) and the precedences. (3) *A hand-written scanner in C*, with state it serializes, for what no regular lexer can do: indentation, heredocs, raw strings, nested template literals.

The limitation is what none of the three has: a symbol table. Tree-sitter cannot know that T was declared a type three screens earlier, so it decides T * x; by precedence and by which reading survives, not by what the program declared, and where the two readings are both valid it picks by a number the grammar author chose. That is why Tree-sitter's C and C++ trees are sometimes wrong where it matters (a typedef name taken for a variable), and why a tool that needs the right answer must run its own semantic pass over the tree afterwards. It is also the price of what Tree-sitter is for: incremental re-parsing and a tree for text that is half-written, where a wrong guess is cheap and an error is not allowed.

The other cost is the machinery. In the repository's benchmark (bench/results/treesitter.md) on one toy language at 100,000 rules, Tree-sitter parses the 7.7 MB file in 226 ms with 63 MB of memory, against hbnf's C parser at 26 ms and 21 MB; at a million rules, 2,302 ms and 620 MB against 266 ms and 204 MB. That is about nine times the time and three times the memory, on a grammar with no ambiguity in it, so the forking itself is not in the number: it is the price of the tables, the tree it builds for editing, and the generality of the lexer.

hbnf's forks (decision 14) are a smaller thing than GLR and should not be confused with it. They are depth-first: try one alternative to the end of the enclosing sequence and, if something later in that sequence fails, back up and try the next, with no stack of stacks and no reach past the end of a rule. Where Tree-sitter keeps both readings alive and decides at the end, hbnf's contexts decide at the choice: a ? types test on an alternative removes the reading that the declared names rule out before anything is parsed under it. That is the part of C++ Tree-sitter cannot do, and the part of C++ that forks are not needed for.

There is a Tree-sitter backend (--backend=tree-sitter), and what it shows is the same difference from the other side. A HyperBNF grammar becomes a grammar.js mechanically, ALGOL 60 and JSON parse the same texts in Tree-sitter as in HyperBNF (JSON: 2,000 documents and 2,000 mutations, no difference; ALGOL 60: every one of 500 sentences of the Report), but ordered choice has to be turned into conflicts and prec.dynamic (four conflicts for ALGOL 60), and a lookahead, a context test or a back-reference cannot be turned into anything: it is left out, and the nine mutants out of 500 that only Tree-sitter accepted were all the lookahead that was left out. The clearest case is Mustache, whose closing tag must repeat the opening one ({{#a}} ... {{/a}}): hbnf says it with as and $K, and the Tree-sitter grammar it writes accepts {{#a}}x{{/b}}, because a Tree-sitter grammar cannot make a token repeat another; 106 of 2,000 mutated templates were accepted by Tree-sitter alone, and 103 of those 106 have a closing tag naming another section. Tree-sitter's one place for state is the external scanner, a C file with state of its own that Tree-sitter saves and restores for every fork, and the backend uses it (--ts-scanner=FILE): the scanner holds the name the opening tag captured and accepts the closing tag only if it is the same text. With it the 106 disagreements are gone (0 of 2,000). The same scanner does the lookahead !x and &x (a zero-width token that looks at the text, skipping the white space the grammar skips, and takes the literal that follows it into the test, because Tree-sitter cannot take a token back): the nine ALGOL 60 mutants only Tree-sitter accepted are gone too, and 3,000 sentences and 3,000 mutations of the Report agree exactly. What the scanner cannot do is see the parse, only the characters, so a context test that depends on what the parser has reduced (whether T was declared a type) still has no equivalent.

The thread through all of it: Backus and Naur gave a generative notation, Johnson mechanized the LR subset of it, Crocker loosened the notation for protocol writers, Parr and then Myers walked production parsers back to debuggable top-down code, Ford replaced generative choice with ordered recognition, and Brunsfeld optimized the tree for a buffer that is never finished.

It is a pendulum between the two readers, each swing trading one reader's ease for the other's.

HyperBNF

HyperBNF starts from where that thread left off and picks a side. Its bet is that the notation worth compiling is the one the RFCs already use — ABNF — and that a grammar should be documentation first and a parser second. The same file that explains a language to a reader compiles to a parser and a typed tree in C, Rust, Zig, or Ada, self-contained, with no runtime library. (A grammar that chooses the tape layout includes one header of C, runtime/hbnf_tape.h; the others need nothing.)

It keeps the best of each system and dodges the worst, in the way Jeff Fox taught at UltraForth (may he rest in peace): do not solve the hard problem, remove what makes it hard. Ordered choice leaves no shift/reduce conflict to resolve. Left recursion read as a loop leaves nothing to rewrite. A shadowed alternative refused up front leaves no ambiguity to stumble on at runtime. Each system's pain is not fought; it is not built in.

Where it succeeds. An RFC grammar compiles nearly verbatim: =, |, /, *, 1*, n*m, [ ], and the core character classes all mean what an RFC reader already expects. Character-level rules compile to scanners — int = 1*DIGIT, comment = "#" *comment_char — so there is no separate lexer grammar and no regex. Direct left recursion is read as a loop, not rejected. Ordered choice keeps conflicts impossible, and a shadowed alternative is refused at schema time with a diagnostic rather than failing at runtime. The tree is typed from the shape of the rule — enum, scalar, list, struct, and for a choice among rules a union with the number of the alternative taken (_alt) — with no visitor to write. And HyperBNF makes round-tripping easy: comments are first-class tokens, not whitespace, and a value written 0xFF comes back as 0xFF, not 255, so a grammar that wants it — obconf does — hands the file back unchanged, and pretty-printing and debug printouts become simple and pleasant. A parse failure is a line, a column, and a caret under the token that was expected.

The literal syntax is C's on purpose: a string is "…", a character 'a', a code point 0x21 or 33, an escape \n. The reader already knows these spellings, and a notation should astonish no one — every mark it invents is one more thing to learn before the grammar is readable. The choice is not cosmetic. At the root a token is a sequence of bits, and nothing else; 0x21 and "!" name the same byte, so a grammar can reach straight down to the bytes it describes instead of floating above them in a lexer's abstractions. Human readability and machine exactness meet there: spell the bits the way a person already spells them, and keep the design honest about what is underneath.

The work of 28 to 31 October 2026. A parser can now build what its author wants as it reads: %emit { } blocks and rules that say their type (name :: type = ...) write a value, not a tree, and rollback { } names what the emits change so that a retry puts it back. JSON, TOML and YAML were written that way onto a flat tape of words (corpus/*/*-tape.hbnf, runtime/hbnf_tape.h), with number and string conversion in runtime/hbnf_scalar.h, checked bit for bit against strtod and timed against fast_float. A layout turned that into a choice made once for a whole grammar: ast (the default), tape, tailq and rb are directories, layouts/NAME/layout.hbnf, and the daemons' lists become their own TAILQs and red-black trees. Context parameters let one rule stand for a pair (declarator(C in "vars" | "types")). Six grammars of C, from 32V's of 1979 to C11, are measured against the yacc grammars they replace and tested on the SQLite amalgamation. The C arena maps its chunks and goes back for the iteration that ends a list, and a list stores its head alone.

Alternatives that begin alike are now read once: the generator works out a tree of shared beginnings once (HBNF_Lookahead.Factor), and the C, Rust, Zig and Ada backends all walk it; / accepts such pairs, and --lint says where a | could be a /. A ladder of binary operators looks once past its first operand. Last, the templates that the code generators fill are Mustache, and the Mustache renderer is the project's own: HBNF_Template reads a template with the parser hbnf makes of grammars/template.hbnf, so the generator now depends on a parser it generated, and the external mustache-ada library it used from 3 October is gone.

Where it could do better. It has no incremental parse; an edit means parsing the file again. It has no error recovery; bad input fails instead of producing a broken tree. An alternation that mixes keywords with rules gets no kind discriminant in the C tree, so a consumer must infer which branch matched. The drop-in for parse.y (a parse_config the grammar writes in its epilogue around the statement driver) is C-only; the other backends warn and parse the whole file. And the grammar must be LL(1)-ish under maximal munch — it will not decide an ambiguous branch by looking arbitrarily far ahead.

Where it will not. Two things are off the table, and they are the same thing. Incremental, error-recovering parsing is Tree-sitter's entire reason for existing, and it requires a generalized-LR engine with a stack of stacks. HyperBNF's design is ordered choice plus maximal munch, the opposite trade; adding GLR would mean discarding the design. And HyperBNF will not handle genuinely ambiguous grammars, because that is not a missing feature to add later — it is the thing the design refuses on purpose. ALL(*) and GLR exist for the languages that need them. HyperBNF exists for the ones that do not, and for the person who wants the grammar and the parser to be the same document.

Judged on that single aim — one document that serves both the person and the machine — the others all pick a side. BNF and ABNF are for people; Yacc and Tree-sitter are for machines; ANTLR and PEG generate code but drop the writer's comments and exact spellings. HyperBNF tries to be both at once, and the round trip a grammar can build is the proof: the text a person reads is what the machine runs, and the machine hands it back unchanged. The writer's intention arrives whole, and no earlier tool managed that.

The line ends where it began, and it ends against Chomsky. Generative grammar started as a claim about the mind: a language is a set of rules that produces its sentences. That method drove the early years — BNF and Yacc both generate. But the useful work drifted the other way. ABNF defines a language without an automaton; PEG replaces generation with ordered recognition; the grammar stopped producing strings and started describing the ones that exist. Generation lost. Description won.

Isaac Mozeson would be pleased. Chomsky was cruel to him — an Orthodox Jewish linguist who described the actual words, tracing every tongue back to a single source, and who saw language as the thing that brings people together — and Chomsky dismissed him as he dismissed everyone who disagreed. The field came around to his side anyway. The descriptive method won, and it won pointing the way he pointed: a grammar that names what is there can join a person to a machine as readily as it joins one person to another. Chomsky was nasty, and Chomsky was wrong. Bringing people and machines together through language is the more useful end.

The sciences of the mind are following. The scientific consensus is shifting: Chomsky's idea of an innate, rigid "grammar engine" in our DNA is losing ground to probabilistic neural prediction. But while an LLM is a flat next-token predictor, the human brain is a structural predictor — calculating what comes next while simultaneously keeping track of the overall meaning and mental map of the conversation. The recent work behind this comes from Cornell University, the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and New York University. At the Hebrew University, Ariel Goldstein and colleagues recorded the brain during a podcast and found its processing steps follow the layers of GPT-2 and Llama 2, with phonemes and morphemes explaining the activity less well than the models' contextual representations. At Cornell, Yngwie Nielsen and Morten Christiansen ran four priming experiments with 497 participants and found that what speakers know is not explained by constituents as traditionally construed: the commonest three- and four-word sequences, such as "can I have a", are often not constituents, yet they seem to be part of what speakers know. Reports of the study take care over what it shows, something missing from the account and not that the account is wrong, and note that generative theory has changed a great deal since 1957. At New York University, David Poeppel's group found the brain's word predictions shaped by the grammatical grouping of words into phrases, a sensitivity language models do not show: the brain predicts like a model, and it predicts with structure. A grammar is that structure written down, a description of the language as its speakers have it, which is the side Mozeson was on.

An intelligence that must read what people wrote and act on it needs exactly that bridge — a text a person can read, a machine can run, an intention carried whole. HyperBNF was not written for AI. That is what will make it useful to AI.

Sources: the hbnf repository, and RFC 5234 for the ABNF it compiles. For the studies of the brain: Goldstein et al., "Temporal structure of natural language processing in the human brain corresponds to layered hierarchy of large language models", Nature Communications (2025; reported January 2026) · Nielsen and Christiansen, "Evidence for the representation of non-hierarchical structures in language", Nature Human Behaviour (online 21 January 2026) · Zou, Ding and Poeppel, "Constituent-constrained word prediction during language comprehension", Nature Neuroscience (2026; doi:10.1038/s41593-026-02272-6).

Leverage: a notation for people is a notation for models

HyperBNF is built on one premise: what lessens a person's mental load lessens a language model's token usage. It is an application of leverage. A small text, read once, does the work of many lines, and the reader, whether a person or a model, pays for what it has to read and write.

The lever is the grammar. A person who reads the description of JSON as a few rules holds the whole language in mind; a person who reads it as hundreds of lines of hand-written parser does not. A model is in the same position and is charged for it by the token: it reads what the grammar says and writes what it changes, and every symbol of either costs. Measured, below, counts that cost in symbols, characters and the tokens of two public tokenizers, for the published grammar of each language against the same language in HyperBNF.

Reliability is the other half, and the engine around a model decides it as much as the model does. In October 2026 Jesse Donkervliet and Junhao Li of Ubicloud reported that Kimi K2.6 served by vLLM 0.28 gave "the expected tool call schema only 92% of the time". They had carried their own fork of vLLM to reach 100% tool-call schema accuracy, then switched to the constrained decoding that vLLM added upstream in v0.25, and found their score "dropped back to 91%": vLLM "changed its default behavior for requests that do not explicitly turn constrained decoding on". Enabling it and re-running the K2VV ToolCall benchmark gave 100% across all the served model versions, and "this runtime enforcement adds negligible performance overhead". Their conclusion: "When using LLMs, the quality you observe isn't based on the model alone. The inference engine also plays an important role."

Constrained decoding is a grammar at work. At each step the engine lets the model choose only among the tokens that keep the output valid, so a schema stops being something the model usually follows and becomes something it cannot leave. What the engine consumes is a grammar: vLLM builds it with structural tags from the XGrammar library. A model predicts one token at a time; the grammar supplies, from outside, the structure a flat predictor lacks.

HyperBNF is a notation for that grammar which already compiles to parsers in four languages. One file can then give the program its parser and the decoder its grammar, so that the program reads exactly what the model was allowed to write and the two cannot drift apart, unlike a daemon's parse.y and an editor's tree-sitter grammar, which are written twice and drift. The emitter for decoders is planned (RFCPLAN.md, step 14) and not built, and nothing measured in this document shows a gain in a model's accuracy from HyperBNF itself.

Sources: Donkervliet and Li, "Fixing Tool-Call Accuracy in vLLM", Ubicloud, 20 October 2026.

Appendix — Incremental Parsing without Giving Up the Grammar

  1. Tree-sitter's two real wins are incremental reparse and error recovery. Both

rest on a concrete tree whose nodes carry byte spans: an edit reuses every subtree it does not touch, and a broken file still yields a tree with ERROR and MISSING nodes instead of nothing.

  1. Tree-sitter needs generalized LR for this because it must preserve ambiguity

across an edit. HyperBNF has no ambiguity to preserve, because it refuses it up front. A deterministic parser has exactly one result at a position, so an edit invalidates only the nodes whose span it touches; the rest is reused as-is, and re-parsing the touched range is guaranteed to match what a full parse would have produced. Incremental parsing falls out of determinism, not out of a stack of stacks.

  1. Recovery is the same trick in the other direction. Where the parser fails it

does not fork; it writes an ERROR node, skips to the next synchronizing token, resumes, and writes MISSING where a token was expected. The valid regions stay correct because the core parser never changed. Recovery is a wrapper, not a replacement.

  1. The grammar stays the document. The extended ABNF — =, |, /, *,

1*, n*m, [ ], the character classes, the %scan{} scanner actions, whitespace ws — remains the single source, read by the person and run by the machine. The spans and the recovery are added underneath, invisible in the notation.

  1. One thing is worth taking back, lost when ABNF dropped it: the angle

brackets. Naur's 1960 report wrote <expression> for a non-terminal and the terminal bare, and Knuth kept the convention in *The Art of Computer Programming* and WEB. ABNF threw the brackets away to fit ASCII. HyperBNF already reads = and ::=, | and /, side by side, so reading <name> as sugar for name is the same move, and it is the one a literate grammar wants — the bracket marks the thing being defined for the reader, and the parser ignores it. The brackets are for non-terminals. That was the point: a terminal is written as itself, a non-terminal is written as a name in brackets, and the reader tells the two apart at a glance.

Appendix — The people

In the order of the list under the title.

Emil Post (1943). Emil Leon Post (1897–1954), a Polish-American logician. His "Formal reductions of the general combinatorial decision problem" (1943) gave production systems — strings rewritten by rules — the mechanism Backus later took for his metalinguistic formulas. He had already shown, independently of Turing and Gödel, the limits of formal computation.

Noam Chomsky (1956). Noam Chomsky (b. 1928), linguist at MIT. Generative grammar turned language from examples into a formal object: a finite rule set that generates the infinite legal sentences. The Chomsky hierarchy — regular, context-free, context-sensitive — is the ladder every parser in this history sits on. That a grammar is a machine for generating strings is his idea.

John Backus (1959). John Warner Backus (1924–2007) led the IBM team that built FORTRAN, then spent the rest of his career arguing that programming should move away from the von Neumann style he had helped entrench. At UNESCO's ICIP in Paris, June 1959, he published the ALGOL 58 syntax as "metalinguistic formulas" derived from Post's production systems — the first finite set of rewrite rules that generated exactly the legal strings. Turing Award, 1977.

Peter Naur (1960). Peter Naur (1928–2016), a Danish astronomer who had used EDSAC for comet orbits and moved to computing at Regnecentralen. As editor of the ALGOL 60 Report (CACM, May 1960) he made the notation usable: angle brackets, ::=, |, with recursion and block structure in the one formalism. Backus wrote a paper; Naur wrote the reference grammar of a real language. Turing Award, 2005.

John McCarthy (1960). John McCarthy (1927–2011), at MIT and later Stanford. Inventor of Lisp; his 1960 paper defined the language with a metacircular evaluator, eval written in Lisp — a program that describes itself, four years before META II. Turing Award, 1971.

Edgar Irons (1961). Edgar T. "Ned" Irons, at Princeton. His "A Syntax-Directed Compiler for ALGOL 60" (January 1961) was the first compiler whose parser ran straight off the BNF, the grammar supplying the recognition tables as a program supplies its data. Then he went further than anyone in this story: he built IMP, a syntax-extensible language whose production rules, written in the program itself, rewrote the compiler's own grammar on the fly — the grammar not merely executed, but evolving as it ran. He used those techniques to build early time-sharing operating systems for the NSA and the Cray supercomputers. He is the hero of this history: the man who showed a grammar could not merely describe a language but run it, and then change it.

R. A. Brooker (1963). Ralph Anthony "Tony" Brooker (1925–2019), at Manchester. With D. Morris he built the Compiler Compiler for the Atlas — the system that gave the field its name. It read a phrase-structure description of a language and generated a machine-code compiler for it; it built compilers for Algol and Atlas Autocode.

D. Morris (1963). Derrick Morris, at Manchester. Co-designer, with Brooker, of the Compiler Compiler.

Christopher Strachey (1963). Christopher Strachey (1916–1975), a British computer scientist. He led the Cambridge–London effort behind CPL (Combined Programming Language, 1963), the ALGOL descendant whose simplifications became BCPL, then B, then C. He went on to found denotational semantics.

Donald Knuth (1964). Donald E. Knuth (b. 1938), at Stanford. He founded the theory this history mechanizes — "On the Translation of Languages from Left to Right" (1965) — and, in The Art of Computer Programming and WEB, the literate ideal that one document can be both prose a person reads and code a machine runs. He insisted the name be Backus–Naur Form, not Backus Normal Form, because it is not a normal form: the honesty of naming, applied to a whole field. Turing Award, 1974.

Dewey Val Schorre (1964). Dewey Val Schorre, at UCLA. META II (1964), the first documented metacompiler: a language written as "syntax equations" in the shape of BNF, each equation compiled to the subroutine that recognized it. He replaced ::= with = and | with /, and introduced $ for repetition because a top-down parser cannot survive left recursion — three decisions every later notation inherited in one form or another. META II compiled itself; he went on to the CWIC compiler-writing project at System Development Corporation.

Robert McClure (1965). Robert M. McClure, at Texas Instruments. TMG (TransMoGrifier, 1965), a recursive-descent compiler-compiler; ported to Unix by McIlroy, it was TMG that Ken Thompson used to write B.

Martin Richards (1966). Martin Richards (b. 1940), at Cambridge. BCPL (Basic CPL, 1966), the typeless language C descended from; his writef is where C's % format directives came from.

Douglas McIlroy (1968). Douglas McIlroy (b. 1932), at Bell Labs. The inventor of the Unix pipe; he implemented TMG on the PDP-7, and it was through his TMG that the compound-assignment operators passed from ALGOL 68 into B and C.

Ken Thompson (1969). Ken Thompson (b. 1943), at Bell Labs. Co-creator of Unix (1969); he set out to write a FORTRAN compiler with TMG and wrote B instead, the language C grew out of. Later Go.

Stephen Johnson (1971). Stephen Curtis Johnson (b. 1944), at Bell Labs. Yacc, lint, and the Portable C Compiler. He wanted to add an exclusive-or to the B compiler and found the hand-written parser impossible to change; Al Aho pointed him at Knuth's LR papers, and Jeff Ullman's "another compiler-compiler?" supplied the name.

Al Aho (1971). Alfred Aho (b. 1941), at Bell Labs and later Columbia. "LR Parsing" (1974, with Johnson) and the "dragon book"; AWK. It was his pointer to Knuth's LR papers that set Johnson on Yacc.

Dennis Ritchie (1972). Dennis Ritchie (1941–2011), at Bell Labs. Co-creator of Unix and the author of C (1972), the language every later system in this history was written in or against.

David Crocker (1982). David H. Crocker, an Arpanet mail practitioner. RFC 822 (1982), the mail-format spec whose section 2 everyone cited as "the BNF in RFC 822" — the citation habit that produced ABNF. IEEE Internet Award, 2004.

Robert Corbett (1985). Robert Corbett. Berkeley Yacc (1985), the LALR engine Bison adopted.

Richard Stallman (1987). Richard Stallman (b. 1953). The C grammar of GCC 1.0 (22 March 1987), fed to Bison; the GNU project and free software.

Michael Tiemann (1987). Michael Tiemann (b. 1964). The g++ C++ front end, the Yacc-based C++ grammar that became the standing lesson in what the machine could not do.

Leonard Tower (1987). Leonard H. Tower Jr. (b. 1949). Parts of the GCC parser, the RTL generator and definitions, and the VAX machine description.

Paul Rubin (1987). Paul Rubin. Most of the GCC preprocessor.

John Gilmore (1987). John Gilmore (b. 1955). Compiled the whole BSD source tree with the VAX GCC in 1987–88 so CSRG could drop pcc — for ANSI C, better code, and a way out from under the AT&T copyright.

Keith Bostic (1987). Keith Bostic (b. 1959), of CSRG. Endorsed the GCC switch that took Berkeley off pcc.

Mike Karels (1987). Michael J. Karels (1956–2024), of CSRG. Endorsed the same switch.

Terence Parr (1988). Terence John Parr, at Purdue and later the University of San Francisco. ANTLR (1988–90), the parser generator that generated the recursive descent he was already writing by hand, with predicates and backtracking for what finite lookahead cannot decide.

Isaac Mozeson (1989). Isaac Elchanan Mozeson (b. 1951), an Orthodox Jewish linguist. *The Word* (1989), his Edenics dictionary, traces words across languages to a single source; he saw language as the thing that brings people together. Chomsky dismissed him. The descriptive method came around to his side anyway, and the studies of the brain are coming around to it too.

Jeff Fox (1996). Jeffrey Arthur Fox (1949–2011), of UltraTechnology. A Forth programmer and a close personal friend of Chuck Moore, the language's inventor; from 1990 they worked side by side on minimal computing taken to its limit: Fox commissioned the F21, a "low fat" chip — a 500 MIPS Forth engine with video, network and analog I/O, a whole workstation on a die he priced at about a dollar. The discipline this document borrows as its epigraph — do not solve the hard problem, remove what makes it hard — is his. May he rest in peace.

Bryan Ford (2002). Bryan Ford, at MIT and later EPFL. Packrat parsing (2002) and Parsing Expression Grammars (2004), recognition-based grammars where ordered choice makes ambiguity impossible.

Anders Magnusson (2002). Anders Magnusson. Revived pcc from the opened 32V sources from 2002, rewriting half the front end and most of the back end, and kept the last Yacc-driven C compiler alive.

Joseph Myers (2004). Joseph S. Myers, a Cambridge mathematician and the long-time GNU C front-end maintainer. Wrote the hand-written recursive-descent C parser that replaced the Bison grammar in GCC 4.1 (2006) — the end of the age of LALR parsing in the world's most visible C compiler.

Max Brunsfeld (2014). Max Brunsfeld, on GitHub's Atom team. Tree-sitter (2014–18), the incremental GLR parser for editors, now the usual answer whenever a tool needs a real syntax tree for a language it does not itself compile.
